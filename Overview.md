# Mosaicus Maximus

Working title. A cozy craft and business game where you run a mosaic workshop in a Roman town. You design new floors for the townspeople and restore ancient floors buried under centuries of dirt. You decide what goes where, and the tiles lay themselves in sweeping animations. Every finished floor stays in the town, and the ones you love go in your library.

There are no timers and no failure.

## The setting

You inherit a dusty workshop from your uncle, a once-famous mosaic maker, in a small town on the coast of Roman Italy. The town is growing, and everyone wants a fashionable floor. Beneath the older houses lie forgotten floors from the town's past, waiting to be uncovered. As your reputation grows, grander clients and grander ruins come your way.

## A day in the workshop

Each day you take one job, or two once you have apprentices. New notices go up on the job board every morning.

**New floors**

1. **A client visits** with a brief and an offer: the room, a theme, colours they love or hate, and what they'll pay. Requests come from real Roman fashions, such as a nervous homeowner who wants a guard dog and "Cave Canem" by the door, a bathhouse that wants an octopus, or a rich host who wants the floor to look like scraps from a feast were dropped on it.
2. **Compose the design** at the design table, using motifs from the pattern book.
3. **Choose the stones.** Each material has its own colour, price and look.
4. **Lay the floor** at the client's house, one stone at a time. You pick a stone from the tray, and the places it goes glow faintly in its colour; sweeping the brush over them lays that stone and no other, and hundreds of tiles cascade into place, following the design and bending around curves the way Roman rows did. When a stone is used up, you pick the next.
5. **Grout and polish:** pick the sponge and wipe the grout into the gaps, then pick the cloth and polish the grouted stone until it shines.
6. **The reveal.** The client sees the finished room, gives it one to five stars, reacts and pays.

**Restorations**

1. **An old floor is found** under a house being rebuilt, a collapsed villa or the old baths, hidden under soil, soot, roots and later plaster.
2. **Clean it** with the trowel. The dirt lies thick in places and worn thin in others, so parts of the picture already show.
3. **Repair it** one tile at a time: pick the stone that matches a gap and click to set it, and lift out a wrong one with the chisel. Press Done when every gap is filled. A close colour match earns a better payment, and the floor is shown and kept exactly as you mended it.
4. **Copy the old pattern.** Most old floors hold a pattern nobody in town has seen in years. Restoring one adds it to your pattern book, so digging is the way to new designs before the next chapter brings them.

**Copies**

A collector brings a painted sketch of a famous floor and wants it copied. The sketch is pinned beside the plan, with a likeness meter showing how close your copy is. Holding the pointer over the sketch lays it across your plan to compare. The client pays for likeness, not looks.

**The town contest**

Every seventh day the town holds a floor contest. There is no client and no brief: the judges score how the floor looks, and the rival workshop enters too. Beating the rival wins a prize and a lot of reputation.

**Festivals**

Every tenth day is a Roman festival, in turn the Floralia, Neptunalia, Vinalia, Consualia and Saturnalia. A letter in the morning says which. That day the townsfolk's notices ask for the festival's theme (flowers, the sea, wine and food, the races, a feast) and pay a quarter more, and the first floor of four stars or more laid for the festival wins the festival prize and some reputation.

**Picture panels**

Once a day, between jobs, you can lay a small picture panel at the workbench, as Roman workshops made their emblemata. It is designed and laid like a small square floor. A collector buys it at the workbench, for more the more stars it earns, or you can set it into a client's floor or a contest entry from the Pictures page of the pattern book. A panel set into a floor keeps its size and stones, comes already laid, needs no stone bought for it, and the client pays extra for it.

**Wall mosaics**

In the last chapter, the baths, the temple and the rich ask for wall mosaics too: an arched fountain niche, which is designed and laid like a floor and suits glass and gold. At the reveal it is shown in its niche on the wall.

**The dog finds digs**

Some mornings the dog comes home covered in dirt, having found an old floor at the bottom of a hole next door. A letter says so, and the dig goes up on the job board with a paw on its notice; it always hides a pattern.

Restorations grow in size and importance, ending with the grand floor buried beneath the forum.

## The business

- **How a floor is judged:** a client first checks their wishes, such as a guard dog or no red marble. Then they look at the floor itself, in five things anyone can see:
  - **Symmetry:** whether the floor mirrors itself, with pictures on the middle line or in matching pairs.
  - **A centrepiece:** one big piece in the middle that catches the eye.
  - **How full it is:** neither bare nor crowded, and filled evenly.
  - **Colours:** a handful that stand out from the ground, not one and not ten.
  - **A frame:** a border round the edge.

  The design table shows the stars a floor will earn while you work on it, a tick or cross for each of the five, and the next thing to fix. Dotted lines on the plan mark the room's middle, side to side and top to bottom. Pay, tips and reputation all rise with the stars.
- **Tastes:** every client also has a taste, such as loving a mirrored floor, wanting it busy, wanting it calm, loving colour or wanting a strong border. Townsfolk say theirs when they visit. The named clients don't: you find out from what they say at the reveal, and from then on their taste is written in your notebook and counts on the design table's stars.
- **The notebook** on the job board lists every named client you've met, what you know of their taste, and how far their story has gone.
- **Merchant ships** put into the harbour every few days with a stone the town can't usually get. The stone merchant sells it that day only, and once you've bought it, it's yours to use from then on.
- **Choosing jobs:** offers differ in pay, size, difficulty and how much the job does for your reputation.
- **Setting the price:** you name your price by sliding the coins, up to about one and a half times their budget. A higher price raises the client's expectations. Ask too much and the client names their own price instead, and the slider moves to it; ask too much a second time and they leave for your rival.
- **The rival workshop** competes with you for the big jobs and poaches clients who leave unhappy.
- **Word of mouth:** a great floor brings the client's friends and neighbours. Each client's requests follow a small story of their own, such as a feud, a wedding or a new fortune.
- **Costs:** stone, apprentice wages and workshop upgrades. A floor is laid only from stone in stock, bought in sacks of 100 at the stone merchant's shelves; the design table lists any stone a floor is short of and can buy just those sacks, and what is left over stays in stock.
- **Apprentices** let you take a second job each day.
- **The day passes** while you play, from morning to night, as in Farmus Maximus. The sky through the door and window turns from blue to gold to stars, the room's light follows it, and the lamps flicker and light the room after dark. A day ends when you choose to end it; the job board says so plainly once the day's jobs are done.

## Materials and techniques

Progress follows the real history of Roman mosaics, from simple to spectacular, in four chapters:

1. **Black and white:** limestone and black stone in geometric patterns, like the floors of ordinary Roman homes.
2. **Coloured stone:** marbles in reds, greens and ochres, and terracotta, for borders, waves and simple figures.
3. **Picture panels:** tiny tiles for detailed pictures, such as portraits, animals and myths, set into the centre of a floor.
4. **Glass and gold:** coloured glass and gold-leaf tiles for walls, fountains and the town's new temple.

Every finished floor earns reputation, and better floors earn more; enough reputation opens the next chapter. The chapters are never hidden. The top bar's Chapter button shows the chapter you're in and your reputation against the next one, and opens a list of every chapter with what each brings: its stone, patterns, borders, rooms, clients and upgrades. Each new chapter opens with a letter listing what it adds, and the reveal and the end of the day say how much reputation the next chapter still needs.

**Achievements** (Steam achievements too, when the game runs through Steam) mark finished floors, five-star floors, chapters, digs, contest wins, near-perfect copies, each client's story, money earned, a floor of ten thousand tiles, stone off a ship, petting the dog and the library. A card with a laurel slides in when one is earned, and the settings list them all.

The pattern book fills with real Roman designs as you unlock them, such as the key pattern, braided borders, rosettes, dolphins, birds and Medusa's head.

## Placing tiles

This is the moment the whole game is built around, so every tile has to feel good, and a sweep of hundreds has to feel better.

**The animation**

- **Each tile drops in:** a tile appears just above the floor, slightly too big, with its shadow underneath. It falls, lands with a small squash, bounces once and settles, snapping to its final angle with a tiny puff of mortar dust. It glints for a moment as it lands.
- **Sweeps ripple:** tiles don't all land at once. They fall in a wave outward from the brush, a few milliseconds apart, so a sweep looks like a ripple running across the floor.
- **Finishing a motif:** when the last tile of a dolphin or a border goes down, a shimmer runs across it from end to end.
- **Finishing the floor:** a wave of light crosses the whole floor, then the camera pulls back to show it whole.
- **Grouting:** the sponge works like a squeegee. Dusty, dull tiles turn clean in a stripe behind it.
- **Polishing:** the cloth leaves a wet-looking shine, with sparkles where the light catches the glass and gold.
- **Cleaning old floors:** dirt and plaster break off in flakes and crumbs. A splash of water darkens the stone and shows its true colours for a moment before it dries.
- **Repairs:** when a gap is filled, the crack around it closes with a brief golden line.
- **Steam Deck:** a soft rumble with each wave of tiles.

**The sound**

- **Each stone has its own voice:** marble clicks hard, limestone gives a softer tock, terracotta a dull clay knock, glass a bright clink, and gold a tiny bell-like ping. Each has many recorded variations with slight changes in pitch, so no two landings sound the same.
- **No noise:** fast sweeps sound like rain on stone, not a racket. Overlapping clicks are thinned out and the volume is kept even.
- **No harp while you work:** laying, grouting and polishing make only the sounds of stone, sponge and cloth. The harp sweep is kept for the reveal.
- **Everything else:** grout squelches, sponges squish, polish squeaks, chisels tap, plaster crumbles, water splashes, and coins pour into your purse.

## What it looks like

The workshop, town and people are warm pixel art in the style of Farmus Maximus: terracotta roofs, cream plaster, cypress trees and Mediterranean light. Scenes are drawn at 480 by 270 and scaled up to fill the screen.

The floors are drawn by code. Each tile is a tiny pixel-art stone a few pixels wide, slightly irregular in shape, with its own shading and an occasional chip, set in grout lines. You can zoom in to see single stones and out to see the whole floor, and tiles stay crisp at every zoom level.

The palette comes from real Roman stone: cream limestone, black, terracotta red, ochre, green marble, glass blues and gold. The menus look like wax tablets, wooden boards and pattern-book pages, with a small pixel font.

## The screens

There are eight screens. The workshop is the hub, and every other screen opens from it.

1. **The workshop (side view).** You see a single room straight on, like a stage, painted as one picture so every piece shares the window's light. On the left is the open street door to the town. The job board hangs on the wall beside it, and a rack of scrolls by the window holds your library. Shelves of stone sacks stand against the back wall, the workbench sits under the window with its tools on pegs above it, and the design table stands at the front of the room. Your dog sleeps in its basket and gets up, wagging, when you pet it. You choose the dog when you start, from the same breeds and coats as Farmus Maximus, and can change it in the settings. Hovering a piece lights it with a thin gold outline; clicking it opens its screen. Upgrades add pieces to the room, such as potted plants, wine jars and lamps. A slim bar along the top shows your coins, the chapter with your reputation, and the day.
2. **The job board.** A wooden board fills the screen, pinned with wax-tablet notices. Each notice shows the client's portrait, the room, a size marker, the pay and the reputation it's worth. Restoration notices carry a rough charcoal rubbing of the little that shows through the dirt. Picking a notice brings the client in. A notebook on the board keeps what you know about each named client.
3. **The client visit.** The client stands at your counter in the workshop, with a large pixel portrait and a speech bubble. Their wishes collect into a brief card: the theme, the colours they want and don't want, and the budget. You set your price by sliding a stack of coins across the counter, and their face reacts as you push. Then you shake on it or turn them away.
4. **The design table (top-down).** You look straight down at the desk. On the left is the pattern book, open, with tabbed pages for borders, patterns, pictures and letters. On the right is the room's floor plan on a wax tablet, in the room's real shape with its doorways. You drag motifs from the book onto the plan and resize them by dragging their corners, down to a few tiles across. To recolour, you click any part of a motif on the plan, or its part in the row under the plan, and that part glows; then you pick a stone. Turning, mirroring, pairing and removing sit on a small bar that floats above the chosen motif. The brief card is pinned in the corner, and each wish gets a tick as you meet it. Undo and redo take back any change. Under the brief card are the stars the floor will earn, a bar for each of the five things clients look at, and a hint about the weakest one; for a copy it shows the sketch and the likeness instead. The total stone cost adds up underneath.
5. **The floor (top-down, at the client's house).** You look down at the room's bare mortar bed, with your design sketched on it in faint red chalk, as Roman craftsmen did. The room's walls frame the edges, and the client's family sometimes peeks in at the door. Along the bottom is a tray with what the job in hand needs: your stones, with how many of each are left to lay, or the trowel, the chisel, the sponge and the cloth. Nothing in it is picked until you pick it.
6. **The dig (top-down).** This is the same view for restorations, but the floor is buried. The tools are the trowel for soil, the chisel for plaster, the brush for dust and the sponge for washing, and then the same repair tools as a new floor. A meter shows how much of the floor is uncovered.
7. **The reveal.** The finished floor is shown in the client's own room: a painted cutaway seen from above the front, like a dollhouse, with the room's walls and furniture (a desk and scroll cupboard in a study, couches in a dining room, an altar in the temple) and your floor laid in it, while a band of light runs across the new stone. There are no people in the room. The client comes in on the left with their stars and what they think, coins pour into your purse, and the brief card shows what you met. A restoration also shows the pattern it added to your book, and a contest shows the judges' verdict against the rival. One button keeps the floor in your library.
8. **The town map.** This is a painted pixel-art map of the town from above, reached through the street door. It shows the harbour, the forum, the baths and the villas on the hill. Every house with one of your floors is marked, and clicking it shows your floor in its room, painted as at the reveal, with its stars and what its owner thought of it. The rival workshop and active digs are marked too. New districts open as your reputation grows.

**The library** opens from the scroll rack in the workshop and from the title screen. It is a shelf of every floor you kept, from any workshop and from free design. Choosing one shows it large, and you can watch it being laid again, tile by tile in the order you laid it, with its sounds. A button copies the picture and a time-lapse GIF to a folder in your Pictures, for sharing.

The stone merchant, the workbench (picture panels and workshop upgrades) are panels that open over the workshop screen, not separate screens. Free design uses the design table and floor screens, with an empty villa room and no client.

## Modes

- **Story:** the town's jobs, its characters and their gossip, through to the temple and the floor beneath the forum.
- **Free design:** any size of floor with every material, and no client. Available from the start.

## Music

The music is calm harp, which softens briefly under big moments.

## Platform and controls

Steam, on Windows, Mac, Linux and Steam Deck. The game is played mainly with the mouse, with full controller support for the Deck. It's built in Godot 4 in 2D.
